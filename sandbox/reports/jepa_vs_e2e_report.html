CANDI: JEPA vs End-to-End Reconstruction

How the two-stage JEPA pipeline separates imputation from decoding, and why that matters for epigenome denoising.

1. The Core Idea at a Glance

CANDI takes raw sequencing read counts for epigenomic assays and experimental metadata (sequencing depth, assay type, read length, run type), and predicts the full signal for all 35 assays — including those that were never measured for a given cell type.

There are two fundamentally different ways to train this system:

End-to-End Reconstruction-Only

One monolithic training pass. The encoder, decoder, and output heads all receive gradients from the reconstruction loss (how well the outputs match the actual signal). Every component is shaped by the signal-space objective.

Stage 1 + Stage 2 JEPA Pipeline

Two decoupled stages. First train the encoder + predictor to work in latent space only (no signal reconstruction). Then freeze them and train lightweight decoders to translate latent representations back to signal space.

The key separation: In JEPA, the predictor is responsible for imputation and denoising (going from observed context to predicted target, in latent space). The decoder's job is only to translate the predictor's output back to signal space. Metadata conditioning happens at the predictor level, not at the decoder.

Where metadata conditioning happens

In end-to-end CANDI, FiLM layers inject metadata at every convolutional layer in both the encoder and decoder. The decoder is not a pure spatial upsampler — it's a metadata-conditioned function at every stage. This means the decoder must simultaneously learn (a) how to upsample and (b) what the target assay should look like — entangling the two concerns.

The predictor learns: "given what I can see (context), and told what the target experiment is (metadata), predict what the encoder would produce from the full input."
L_stage1 = MSE(z_pred, proj_tgt) + λ × SIGReg(proj_ctx, proj_tgt)
No signal reconstruction in Stage 1. The encoder learns to produce good latent representations. The predictor learns to impute missing assays and denoise in latent space. SIGReg prevents dimensional collapse by enforcing isotropic covariance in the projection space.

Why metadata conditioning is at the predictor

The predictor receives the target metadata (which assay, at what sequencing depth, etc.) via AdaLN-zero conditioning. This is the critical design choice: the predictor is told "predict what H3K4me3 at 50M reads would look like" and must synthesize that from whatever partial context is available. The encoder never sees which assays are being requested — it only processes what is actually observed.

5. The Predictor: Where Imputation Actually Happens

The most important conceptual shift between end-to-end and JEPA is where the imputation and denoising occur. Let's make this concrete:

End-to-End: Imputation is Diffused
E
Encoder

Encodes whatever assays are available. With FiLM conditioning from metadata at every layer, it partially learns what's missing.

D
Decoder

Must simultaneously: (1) upsample latent → full resolution, (2) use FiLM to figure out what the target assay should look like, (3) handle obs vs imputed quality split. Too many jobs for one component.

Imputation pressure is spread across encoder + decoder. Gradients from 3 heads interfere at the shared encoder.
JEPA: Imputation is Localized
E
Encoder

Encodes what is observed into a clean latent. Shaped by SIGReg for isotropic geometry, not by reconstruction noise.

P
Predictor (the star)

All imputation and denoising happen here. Takes z_ctx + target metadata → z_pred. Conditioned via AdaLN-zero on exactly which assay to predict, at what depth. This is the component that "fills in the blanks."

D
Decoder

Pure latent-to-signal translator. No metadata, no FiLM. z_pred already contains all the information; the decoder just changes the representation format.

Imputation is fully contained in the predictor. Decoder trains independently per head — no interference.

AdaLN-zero modulates the normalization layers with scale (γ) and shift (β) derived from the target metadata. This tells the predictor: "produce the latent for this specific assay at this specific depth."

Think of the predictor as answering the question: "I can see H3K27ac and CTCF for this cell type. What would H3K4me3 at 50M reads look like in latent space?" — and the decoder then simply renders that latent answer into a count track, a signal track, and a peak call track.

7. What Each Paradigm Gets Right

End-to-End Strengths

✓ Simplicity — one training loop, one loss, one optimization.

✓ Direct optimization of the metric we care about (signal quality).

✓ No information bottleneck between latent and signal space.

✓ Current best reconstruction quality (B8 baseline: quality_score 5.68).

JEPA Strengths

✓ Clean separation of concerns — imputation vs rendering.

✓ No head interference at the encoder level.

✓ Biologically structured latent space (UMAP clustering by cell type).

✓ Modular — upgrade any stage without retraining others.

✓ Metadata conditioning at the right level (predictor, not decoder).

✓ Latent quality: z_pred is a reusable biological embedding (RNA-seq prediction, etc.).

Current status (May 2026): JEPA produces superior latent representations (biologically structured, isotropic) but the Stage 2 decoder does not yet match end-to-end reconstruction quality (6.29 vs 5.68 quality score). Batch 2 of E28 — fine-tuning the predictor alongside the decoder, one head at a time — is exploring whether the gap is a frozen-predictor limitation or a decoder architecture issue.
